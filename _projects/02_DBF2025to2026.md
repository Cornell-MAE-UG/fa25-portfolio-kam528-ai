The Cornell Design Build Fly (DBF) team competed in the 2026 AIAA Design/Build/Fly competition, which challenged university teams to design, manufacture, and test a fixed-wing aircraft capable of completing multiple mission profiles, including payload transport and aerial banner towing. The aircraft had to balance aerodynamic efficiency, structural integrity, manufacturability, and flight performance while satisfying strict competition constraints.

As a member of the Aerodynamics Team, my primary responsibilities focused on the design, analysis, and manufacturing of the wing and control surface assemblies. I contributed to the CAD design of the wings and ailerons, performed CFD simulations on the wings and nose cone, completed structural FEA on the carbon fiber wing spar, and helped manufacture multiple aircraft iterations that were flight tested throughout the competition season.

The competition required a single aircraft to successfully complete multiple flight missions while remaining lightweight, structurally reliable, and aerodynamically efficient. The aircraft needed to carry payloads, tow and release an aerial banner, and maintain stable flight characteristics under varying loading conditions.

Because aerodynamic performance, structural strength, and manufacturability are closely linked, every design decision required balancing multiple engineering constraints. The wings, control surfaces, and carbon fiber spar were particularly critical, as they directly influenced lift generation, aircraft stability, and structural integrity during flight.

<a id="cfd-results"></a>
<h1>CFD Results</h1>

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed in ANSYS Fluent to evaluate the aerodynamic performance of the wing and nose cone. Pressure contours, velocity contours, and airflow streamlines were analyzed to identify regions of flow separation and verify that the aircraft maintained efficient airflow characteristics.


<a id="fea-results"></a>
<h1>FEA Results</h1>

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) was performed on the carbon fiber wing spar using ANSYS Mechanical to evaluate structural performance under aerodynamic loading. The analysis focused primarily on maximum deflection of the spar, ensuring sufficient stiffness to maintain wing alignment during flight. Physical spar testing closely matched the simulation results, validating the structural model used during the design process.

<a id="manufacturing"></a>
<h1>Manufacturing</h1>

The wing structure was manufactured using a combination of laser-cut hardwood and balsa wood components. Precision-cut ribs were assembled using interlocking grooves that simplified alignment during construction while maintaining structural accuracy. Hardwood was used in higher stress regions, while balsa reduced weight in less critical areas.

The leading edges were soaked in water before bending to match the required airfoil geometry, while trailing edges were fabricated from shaped balsa wood. Custom 3D-printed components were integrated into the wing center section to improve rib alignment and increase assembly stiffness. Once assembled, the wing was covered with Monokote, and openings were cut to install the control horns and servo linkages.

Following early aircraft iterations, additional 3D-printed alignment fixtures were designed for the wings, tail, and control surfaces. These fixtures significantly improved manufacturing repeatability by controlling component alignment during assembly and reducing geometric variability between aircraft.

<a id="results"></a>
<h1>Results</h1>

Over the course of the competition season, four aircraft iterations were designed, manufactured, and flight tested. Each aircraft provided valuable insight that guided subsequent design improvements.

The first aircraft was lost following a hard landing caused by landing gear failure. The second aircraft successfully completed repeated flight testing and banner towing demonstrations before being damaged when the banner became entangled during flight. Challenges encountered during construction of the third aircraft led to the redesign of the wing alignment method and the introduction of custom 3D-printed assembly fixtures, improving manufacturing consistency and reducing assembly variability.

The final aircraft successfully completed all required competition missions at the 2026 AIAA Design/Build/Fly competition. The Cornell team placed **31st overall**, demonstrating the effectiveness of the iterative design, analysis, manufacturing, and testing process.
